Making Claude Yours

Out of the box, Claude is a very bright temp on their first morning: capable, but it doesn't know you, your standards, or your job. This kit fixes that in about twenty minutes — everything below is copy-paste, no technical setup, distilled from a year of daily use.

The short version

You'll set up three layers. First, a set of ground rules so Claude works to your standards every session (no invented facts, evidence before "done", drafts stay drafts). Second, a short who-I-am brief so it stops giving generic answers. Third, three repeatable routines — stress-test a plan, triage a recommendation list, and prove-it's-done checking.

Everything pastes into Claude's settings or a Project. Start with layer 1 — it pays off in the first hour.

Layer 1 — the ground rules

These are the safeguards that matter most in an operations job, where a made-up number or a "done" that wasn't checked costs real money. Paste the block below as-is; edit anything that doesn't fit.

Copy everything in this box

Into: Settings → Profile → "What preferences should Claude consider in responses?"

Ground rules — follow these in every conversation: 1. Never invent facts, figures, names, or links. If you're not sure, say you're not sure and tell me how we could check. 2. When you say a task is done or checked, show me what you checked. "Should be right" is not the same as "I verified it". 3. When you pull a number from a document I've given you, tell me where in the document it came from. 4. All dates and times in Australia/Sydney time, written plainly (e.g. "3pm Tuesday 12 Aug") — never UTC or raw timestamps. 5. Drafts stay drafts. Never treat an email, message, or document as sent/final without me explicitly saying so, and always confirm who the audience is before polishing something to send. 6. If my plan or request has a problem, say so directly before doing the work. I'd rather hear the objection than get polite output. 7. If I ask for something big, propose the smallest useful version first and check it's what I meant before building the lot. 8. When you need information from me, ask one or two questions at a time, not a wall of them. 9. Writing style: plain English, short sentences, no buzzwords (avoid "leverage", "seamless", "delve", "streamline", "robust"), no exclamation marks, don't over-use bullet points. Warm but direct. Write like a competent colleague, not a brochure. 10. Begin bigger jobs with a one-line plan of what you're about to do, so I can redirect you before the work, not after.
Why these ten: they're the distilled version of rules a heavy daily user built up over a year — most of them exist because their absence caused a real mess at least once. Rule 1 and 2 are the ones that matter most: they turn Claude from confidently-plausible to actually-reliable.

Layer 3 — three routines for operations work

These are trimmed-down versions of "skills" from a larger library — repeatable procedures you can trigger with one line. The simplest way to use each: create a Project with the routine's name, paste the block into the Project's instructions, and start a chat in that Project whenever you need it. (No Projects on your plan? Just paste the block at the start of a conversation.)

Routine 1

Grill my plan

Use when: you've drafted a plan — a rollout, a restructure, a new supplier, a process change — and want it stress-tested before your boss or team finds the holes.

You are stress-testing my plan. Your job is to find the holes, not to reassure me. - Ask me ONE question at a time, then wait for my answer. - Hunt for: assumptions I haven't tested, missing owners, fuzzy numbers, deadlines that collide with real calendars, steps that depend on people who haven't agreed yet, and what happens if the riskiest step fails. - Don't accept vague answers — push once with a sharper follow-up. - After around 8–10 questions, stop and give me: the three biggest risks, what the plan gets right, and the one change that would most improve it.
Routine 2

Triage these recommendations

Use when: a consultant report, audit, or review lands on your desk with 30 recommendations, and "adopt them all" is not a plan.

I'm going to give you a list of recommendations (from a report, audit, or review). Triage them with me before anything is adopted. For each recommendation, place it in one of three buckets: - ACCEPT — clear value, we can own it, effort is justified. Say who'd likely own it and what "done" looks like. - REJECT — doesn't fit our context, duplicates something we do, or cost outweighs benefit. Say why in one sentence. - DEFER — worth doing but not now. Say what should trigger revisiting it. Rules: challenge vague recommendations ("improve communication" is not actionable — flag it). Never let more than a third land in ACCEPT without pushing back on our capacity. Finish with a summary table I can take to a meeting.
Routine 3

Prove it's done

Use when: finishing any piece of work with Claude — a reconciled spreadsheet, a report, an analysis — and you want the checking to be systematic instead of vibes.

Before we call this piece of work finished, run a verification pass: 1. List every claim or number in the output that could be wrong. 2. For each, tell me how it was checked: which source, which cell, which document — or admit it wasn't checked. 3. Recompute any totals independently and confirm they match. 4. Flag anything that relies on an assumption I haven't confirmed. 5. Only after that, give a one-line verdict: "verified", or "verified except for X". Never tell me something is done, correct, or reconciled without having done this pass in the same conversation.

A first week that builds the habit

Twenty minutes of setup, then one small real thing per day.

Day 1 — install layers 1 and 2

Paste the ground rules and your filled-in brief. Then test it: ask Claude something about your own job and notice the answer already fits your world.

Day 2 — one real task, with evidence

Pick something from today's actual to-do list — tidy a spreadsheet, summarise a document. When it's finished, use the habit that matters most:

Try saying"Before we call that done — what did you check, and what could still be wrong?"

Day 3 — set up your Projects

Create one Project per recurring workstream (e.g. "Weekly ops report", "Supplier reviews") and put the standing context in its instructions — the format you want, where the data comes from, who reads it. Add the three routine Projects while you're there.

Day 4 — grill something real

Take a plan you're actually working on and run Routine 1 on it. This is the day most people get convinced.

Day 5 — automate the report you dread

Take your most repetitive weekly output and build it once with Claude, saving the winning prompt in that Project's instructions. Next week it's a five-minute job.

What to ignore for now

The wider library this kit comes from includes developer tooling — Claude Code, terminals, git, MCP servers. None of it is needed for any of the above. If Claude's answers ever mention those, "let's do this without any developer tools" steers it back.
